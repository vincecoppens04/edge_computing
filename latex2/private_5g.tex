This chapter explains why Private 5G becomes relevant once computation is no longer placed only at devices or in central clouds, but directly inside the network itself. Earlier in this paper, we noted that workload placement becomes more complex when compute is integrated into the communication infrastructure, as in MEC (Multi-access Edge Computing) and Private 5G environments. Rather than revisiting the technical internals of 5G networking, this section focuses on how such environments reshape the structure and operation of edge systems.

Aether serves as a concrete example of this integration. Instead of treating networking and computation as separate concerns, Aether manages both through a shared, cloud-native control plane \cite{onf_aether_whitepaper}. Reliable edge computing requires predictable communication, while advanced private networks become truly valuable only when they host computation close to where data enters the network \cite{harmatos_5g_tsn}.

In Aether, Private 5G is implemented as a software-defined system. Core network and radio access components are deployed as Kubernetes-orchestrated microservices with declarative lifecycle management \cite{onf_aether_whitepaper}. Updates, restarts and scaling are handled through reconciliation mechanisms rather than manual intervention \cite{onf_aether_whitepaper}.

The architectural relevance becomes most visible in how computation is placed. Edge systems often require processing to occur close to the point where data enters the network, especially when latency and jitter must be tightly controlled. In our own prototype, this proximity was achieved only implicitly by assuming a stable and uncongested local network \cite{edge_computing_report}. Aether formalises this assumption by aligning compute placement with 5G topology. MEC workloads are deployed near the user plane, typically close to radio infrastructure, reducing data-path length and ensuring that latency remains within predictable bounds rather than being subject to best-effort wireless behaviour \cite{onf_aether_whitepaper}.

This placement logic highlights a mutual dependency between Private 5G and edge computing. On the one hand, edge workloads benefit from Private 5G’s ability to provide mobility support, quality-of-service isolation and controlled latency—properties that cannot be structurally guaranteed by Wi-Fi or public mobile networks \cite{private_5g_report, onf_aether_whitepaper}. On the other hand, Private 5G deployments gain much of their value only when paired with edge compute, as many domain-specific tasks such as filtering, analytics and control loops must execute close to the radio layer to avoid unnecessary round trips to central clouds. MEC and Private 5G together form the execution environment for such edge applications.

At the same time, integrating edge computing with Private 5G introduces additional complexity. Spectrum licensing, radio planning and RAN configuration remain inherently physical constraints that software orchestration cannot remove entirely \cite{private_5g_report}. Aether mitigates these challenges through automation and unified management, but does not eliminate them. Its contribution lies in shifting complexity away from ad-hoc deployment decisions into a structured, declarative control plane, enabling edge systems to scale beyond experimental setups while maintaining predictable behaviour \cite{onf_aether_whitepaper}.

Overall, Aether demonstrates that edge computing and Private 5G are not loosely coupled technologies \cite{harmatos_5g_tsn}. Their relationship is architectural rather than incidental: distributed compute requires deterministic communication, and Private 5G becomes operationally meaningful when it supports an edge execution layer. In this model, networking and computation function together as components of a single distributed system spanning radio, edge and application layers \cite{onf_aether_whitepaper}.
