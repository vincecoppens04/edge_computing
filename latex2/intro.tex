This exam paper investigates how three technologies, Kubernetes, AWS Lambda and Private 5G, relate to edge computing in the specific context of the architectural patterns described in our own Edge Computing report \cite{edge_computing_report}. Edge computing shifts computation toward the physical environment, but this shift exposes structural requirements that traditional cloud-native systems do not address on their own. In particular, distributing computation across the edge introduces challenges related to coordination, elasticity and connectivity that become increasingly difficult to manage as scale and heterogeneity grow. These technologies exemplify three distinct architectural concerns in edge systems, distributed orchestration (Kubernetes), elastic cloud-side coordination (AWS Lambda), and deterministic connectivity as an execution substrate (Private 5G), without implying that no other structurally relevant relationships exist.

Kubernetes relates to the operational discipline required in such environments. Our edge prototype already showed that even modest multi-node setups involve configuration management, deployment consistency and workload placement challenges. As the number of edge nodes increases, these issues quickly become unmanageable without automation. The Kubernetes report \cite{kubernetes_report} demonstrates how a declarative control plane and continuous reconciliation provide the mechanisms required to maintain coherence across distributed sites. Kubernetes is therefore essential not merely as a typical orchestration solution, but as the control plane that prevents large-scale edge environments from becoming operationally fragmented.

AWS Lambda addresses a different but equally structural requirement, scalable cloud-side processing for workloads that cannot, or should not, be executed at the edge. Our own pipeline offloaded anomaly data to the cloud, reflecting a broader pattern in edge systems where local computation is combined with centralised reasoning. While local processing reduces latency and bandwidth usage, global aggregation, enrichment and asynchronous workflows still require elastic execution environments. Lambda’s event-driven model offers a principled way to integrate cloud logic into edge architectures without maintaining persistent infrastructure \cite{lambda_report}.

Private 5G is relevant because many edge workloads depend on predictable connectivity. Conventional wireless networks cannot reliably provide the latency bounds, mobility guarantees, or isolation levels required in such settings. As shown in the Private 5G report \cite{private_5g_report}, licensed-spectrum control and local user-plane placement fundamentally alter the performance characteristics of distributed systems. This relationship becomes most visible in architectures where network and compute are tightly integrated, illustrating that the viability of edge computing depends on deterministic communication, while the value of Private 5G becomes most apparent when paired with a structured edge execution layer.

The relationships studied in the following chapters therefore reflect architectural necessity rather than thematic convenience. Each technology addresses a specific limitation that arises when computation moves toward the edge. The chapters analyse these technologies individually, while also highlighting how they interact within a shared architectural context. The paper aims to provide a coherent view of how orchestration, cloud execution and network infrastructure jointly shape edge computing systems.
